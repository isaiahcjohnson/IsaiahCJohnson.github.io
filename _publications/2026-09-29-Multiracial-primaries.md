Multiracial-identified Black candidates are often framed as uniquely positioned coalition builders, able to attract cross-racial support that their Monoracial peers cannot. We test this claim with an original dataset of Black candidates in U.S. House primaries from 2016 to 2024, the first to systematically distinguish Monoracial and Multiracial Black candidates at the congressional level. Using a novel LLM-assisted racial identification methodology validated against a human-coded gold standard, we find that Multiracial candidates raise more money, earn more institutional endorsements, and win primaries at higher rates. Yet this premium is sharply gendered. Multiracial men outperform all groups, winning roughly 67% of their primaries, while Multiracial women win just 33%, the lowest rate of any group. Monoracial women, by contrast, outperform Monoracial men. We explain this reversal with a theory of intersectional gatekeeping, in which donors, party organizations, and identity-based PACs read racial and gender signals together rather than separately. For men, Multiracial identification signals expanded electability. For women, it disrupts a Monoracial credentialing logic that Black-specific institutions consolidated around Black women after 2016. The findings show that the presumed advantages of Multiracial identification are neither universal nor unconditional, with implications for descriptive representation, electoral gatekeeping, and the measurement of racial identity.
